> *In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt wired up the Mark I Perceptron—a room-sized machine of potentiometers and photocells that could learn to tell squares from triangles. Sixty-five years later, the exact same computational atom—weighted sum plus bias plus nonlinearity—scaled to billions of units powers every modern neural network.*

## Key ideas: Building Blocks of Neural Networks

1. **The Artificial Neuron (Affine Map + Nonlinearity)**: Computes $$a = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b)$$, slicing input space with a hyperplane and bending the response through an activation function.
2. **Layered Architectures (Depth vs. Width)**: Stacks neurons into hidden layers that progressively transform raw inputs into abstract, linearly separable latent coordinates.
3. **Activation Functions (Breaking Linearity)**: Prevents multi-layer stacks from collapsing into a single linear matrix while controlling gradient flow during training.
4. **Vectorized Forward Propagation**: Packs entire mini-batches into matrix multiplications $$Z^{[l]} = A^{[l-1]} W^{[l]\top} + \mathbf{b}^{[l]}$$, turning loops over neurons into GPU-friendly BLAS kernels.

### Worked example: One Input Vector Flowing Through a 2-Layer Neural Network

Pass $$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$$ through a tiny $$2 \to 2 \to 1$$ network with ReLU hidden activation and linear output:
$$W^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad W^{[2]} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{[2]} = 0.5$$

1. **Hidden Layer Pre-Activation $$\mathbf{z}^{[1]} = W^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]}$$**:
   $$\mathbf{z}^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(1) + (-1)(-2) + 0 \\ (-2)(1) + 1(-2) + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$$
2. **Apply ReLU Nonlinearity $$\mathbf{a}^{[1]} = \max(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{z}^{[1]})$$**:
   $$\mathbf{a}^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} \max(0, 3) \\ \max(0, -3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Notice Neuron 2 is switched off (gating out negative half-space signals).
3. **Output Layer Prediction $$\hat{y} = W^{[2]}\mathbf{a}^{[1]} + b^{[2]}$$**:
   $$\hat{y} = 2(3) + (-3)(0) + 0.5 = 6.5$$
   Every forward pass in deep learning—no matter how massive—is just this exact affine-then-nonlinear pattern repeated across layers.

Neural Networks are the foundation of modern deep learning. This chapter introduces the basic building blocks of neural networks, including neurons, layers, activation functions, and forward propagation. We'll explore how these simple units combine to create powerful learning systems capable of solving complex problems.

Understanding neural networks is essential for anyone working in deep learning, as they form the basis for more advanced architectures and techniques covered in later chapters.

### Quick intuition: from pixels to predictions

For digit images (e.g. a $$28 \times 28$$ grid), we often **flatten** the image into a vector of $$784$$ numbers and pass it through stacked neuron layers. Each layer transforms the representation; the final layer can produce $$10$$ outputs for digits $$0$$–$$9$$.
