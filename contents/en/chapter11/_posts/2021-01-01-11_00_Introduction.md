Imagine trying to prove you truly understand Rembrandt's brushwork. A critic can tell a genuine Rembrandt from a forgery in seconds—that is *discriminative* modeling—but a master apprentice can sit before a blank canvas and paint a new portrait that belongs in the same gallery. Richard Feynman famously wrote on his final blackboard, *"What I cannot create, I do not understand."* Generative modeling is deep learning's attempt to take Feynman literally: instead of merely drawing decision boundaries between classes, we learn the full probability landscape $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ from which real images, waveforms, molecules, and sentences are drawn.

## Key ideas: The Architecture of Generative Modeling

- **Pillar 1 — Probability Measures & Density Estimation**: Studies how real-world high-dimensional data concentrates on thin, low-dimensional manifolds inside $\mathbb{R}^d$. In this chapter, it frames the core goal: approximating an unknown distribution $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ with a parameterized family $p_\theta(x)$.
- **Pillar 2 — Information Theory & Divergences**: Quantifies the distance between two probability distributions via Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_\theta)$ and Jensen–Shannon divergence. It explains why maximizing log-likelihood is mathematically identical to minimizing forward KL divergence.
- **Pillar 3 — Latent-Variable Factorizations**: Introduces unobserved variables $z$ that explain high-level structure (pose, lighting, speaker identity) so complex joint densities $p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)p(z)\,dz$ become tractable to model and sample.
- **Pillar 4 — Sample Quality vs. Likelihood Evaluation**: Bridges statistical fit (bits-per-dimension, negative log-likelihood) with perceptual realism (FID, Inception Score), explaining why a model with great likelihood can still produce blurry samples and vice versa.

### Worked example: Discriminative vs. Generative Probability on a 2-Pixel Binary Image

Suppose our "images" have just two binary pixels $(x_1, x_2) \in \{0, 1\}^2$, and an empirical dataset of 4 images contains: $(0,0), (0,0), (1,1), (1,1)$.

1. **Empirical data distribution $p_{\text{data}}(x_1, x_2)$**:
   $$p_{\text{data}}(0,0) = 0.5, \quad p_{\text{data}}(1,1) = 0.5, \quad p_{\text{data}}(0,1) = p_{\text{data}}(1,0) = 0.$$
2. **Factorized independent model $q_{\text{ind}}(x_1, x_2) = q(x_1)q(x_2)$**:
   Each marginal has $q(x_i=1) = 0.5$, so $q_{\text{ind}}$ assigns probability $0.25$ to all four states—wasting half its probability mass on impossible patterns $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$. Its average negative log-likelihood (NLL) in bits is:
   $$\text{NLL}(q_{\text{ind}}) = -\frac{1}{2}\log_2(0.25) - \frac{1}{2}\log_2(0.25) = 2.0\text{ bits/image}.$$
3. **Autoregressive generative model $p_\theta(x_1, x_2) = p_\theta(x_1)\,p_\theta(x_2 \mid x_1)$**:
   Setting $p_\theta(x_1=1)=0.5$ and $p_\theta(x_2=1 \mid x_1=1)=0.9$, $p_\theta(x_2=0 \mid x_1=0)=0.9$ yields $p_\theta(0,0) = p_\theta(1,1) = 0.5 \times 0.9 = 0.45$.
   $$\text{NLL}(p_\theta) = -\log_2(0.45) \approx 1.152\text{ bits/image}.$$
4. **Takeaway**: Modeling conditional dependencies cuts the coding cost from $2.0$ bits down to $1.152$ bits (approaching the true entropy $H(p_{\text{data}}) = 1.0$ bit) and ensures $90\%$ of generated samples obey the pixel correlation.

Generative models learn to create new data samples similar to training data. This chapter introduces the fundamentals of generative modeling, probability distributions, maximum likelihood estimation, and evaluation metrics. These concepts form the foundation for autoencoders, VAEs, and GANs.
