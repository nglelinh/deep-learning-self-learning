Why does intentionally sabotaging half of a neural network's neurons on every training step often make the final network *more* accurate on unseen data? Because forcing every neuron to survive random teammate outages prevents fragile memorization and turns a single set of weights into an implicit ensemble of sub-networks. This module hub links the probabilistic theory of Dropout with hands-on PyTorch and NumPy implementations, spatial dropout, and BatchNorm interaction pitfalls.

## Key ideas: Navigating Dropout Regularization

- **Theory & Implicit Ensembles**: Understanding co-adaptation, inverted dropout scaling ($$\frac{1}{1-p}$$), and the connection to adaptive L2 regularization.
- **Code & Diagnostics**: Implementing inverted dropout forward/backward passes from scratch and mastering `model.train()` vs. `model.eval()`.
- **Architecture-Specific Placement**: Choosing dropout rates across MLPs ($$0.2\text{–}0.5$$), CNNs (`Dropout2d`), and Transformers (attention + residual dropout $$0.1$$).

### Worked example: Effective Ensemble Size & Active Parameter Fraction

Consider a 2-hidden-layer MLP where each hidden layer has $$n = 10$$ neurons and dropout rate $$p = 0.5$$.

1. **Number of distinct sub-networks across both hidden layers**:
   $$2^{10} \times 2^{10} = 2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576 \text{ distinct thinned sub-networks!}$$
2. **Expected fraction of weights active in the middle $$10 \times 10$$ weight matrix on any single step**:
   A weight $$W_{ij}$$ is active only if *both* input neuron $$j$$ and output neuron $$i$$ survive:
   $$(1 - p)(1 - p) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25 \quad (\text{only } 25\% \text{ of connections update together}).$$
